Introduction to Chamber Music

The name is a room. Before it was a genre, it was a place: musica da camera, music for the chamber, as opposed to music for the church or the theatre. It was written to be played among friends in ordinary rooms, by people who were often the only audience it would ever have.

There is no conductor. The players must agree, in real time and without an arbiter, on when to breathe, when to press forward, when to yield. Every entrance is a decision someone has to take and the others have to trust. Goethe found the image that has never been bettered:

That is why a village hall on the Dingle Peninsula is not a compromise for this repertoire but its natural home. Nothing is amplified and nothing is hidden. You can hear the rosin, the breath before an entrance, the small sounds of bodies working, and you sit close enough to see a glance pass between two players and then hear what the glance decided.

Antonio Vivaldi

1678–1741

Venice's Red Priest, whose five hundred concertos taught string instruments to sing, and whose La Folia still catches fire.

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart

1756–1791

The most complete musician who ever lived; his last string quintet says goodbye to chamber music with a grin.

Robert Schumann

1810–1856

Poet of the piano and inventor of the piano quintet, whose 1842 masterpiece closes the festival.

Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky

1840–1893

Russia's supreme melodist, who sketched his exuberant string sextet under the Florentine sun.

Anton Arensky

1861–1906

Teacher of Rachmaninoff and devoted disciple of Tchaikovsky, mourned him in a quartet of two cellos and glowing song.

Maurice Ravel

French music's flawless watchmaker, whose Piano Trio was finished in a fever as the Great War began.

Zoltán Kodály

Hungary's musical conscience, who walked village to village saving folk songs, then set them dancing.
